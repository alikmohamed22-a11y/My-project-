Before Nintendo made video games, it made playing cards. Back in 1889.
In Kyoto, Japan, a craftsman named Fusajiro Yamauchi opened a shop making hanafuda: little flower cards, painted by hand.
Card players loved them, and the business kept going for decades.
But by the 1960s, his great-grandson Hiroshi wanted more than cards. A lot more.
So the company tried a taxi service. Instant rice. And, reportedly, even short-stay hotels.
The taxis ran into labor trouble. The rice flopped.
So how did a card company end up in video games?
You see, a young engineer named Gunpei Yokoi built an extending grabber arm, just for fun. The boss saw it, and turned it into a hit toy.
Toys led to electronics. In 1980, Yokoi's Game and Watch put a tiny game in your pocket.
Then, in 1983, the Famicom launched in Japan. And in 1985, the NES reached America.
Here's the twist. Nintendo never stopped making those flower cards. You can still buy them today.
So the company behind your favorite games is still, quietly, a card maker from 1889.
