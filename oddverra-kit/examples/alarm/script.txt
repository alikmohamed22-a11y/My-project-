The first American alarm clock could only ring at one time. Four A.M.
It was seventeen eighty-seven, in Concord, New Hampshire.
A young clockmaker named Levi Hutchins wanted to be up and working before the sun. Every single day.
So he built himself a clock, in a pine cabinet, with a mirror on the door.
Inside, one extra gear rang a bell at four o'clock. Every morning.
Want to sleep in? Too bad. Want to wake up at five? Impossible.
You see, Levi never patented it, and never tried to mass-produce it. He built it just for himself.
It took about sixty years before a French clockmaker, Antoine Redier, patented an alarm you could actually set, in eighteen forty-seven.
Finally, you could pick your own wake-up time.
So America's first alarm clock had no snooze, no settings... and just one opinion about when you should get up.

Facts: Levi Hutchins (b. Harvard, MA, 17 Aug 1761, d. Concord 13 Jun 1855; apprenticed to Simon Willard 1777; clock partnership with brother
 Abel in Concord from 1786): built the clock in 1787, a pine cabinet about 29 x 14 in with a mirror on the door, a brass movement with an
 extra gear that rang a bell at 4 a.m., not adjustable; he wanted to be up before dawn; never patented it or tried to make it in quantity
 (Wikipedia "Levi Hutchins"; NH Magazine "Invented Here"; History.com "Who invented the alarm clock?"; Concord Monitor; Cow Hampshire blog;
 his 1865 Autobiography confirms the family's "early to bed and early to rise" habit, rising at four in winter). Antoine Redier (1817-1892),
 Paris, patented an adjustable mechanical alarm clock in 1847, about 60 years later (History.com; Atlas Obscura "A 2,000-Year History of
 Alarm Clocks"; Bibliotheca Alexandrina SCIplanet). Age not stated in the voiceover (NH Magazine says 26; he turned 26 in Aug 1787).
