The first speeding ticket ever was for going eight miles an hour.
January eighteen ninety-six. Kent, England. Walter Arnold roars through the village of Paddock Wood in his brand-new horseless carriage.
His speed? Eight miles an hour. The limit in town? Two.
So a police constable hops on his bicycle and gives chase, reportedly for five miles.
And he catches him!
In court, Arnold faces four charges, including driving without a horse.
The fine for speeding? One shilling, plus costs.
But that same year, the law changed. The limit jumped to fourteen miles an hour, and drivers celebrated with a run from London to Brighton.
You see, they still drive that run every November.
All because a cop on a bicycle caught a car doing eight.

Facts: 28 January 1896, Walter Arnold of East Peckham, Kent, an early motor dealer (Benz agent; his firm later built "Arnold" motor carriages),
was caught driving at about 8 mph through Paddock Wood where the limit for "locomotives" in town was 2 mph; a constable chased him on a bicycle
(widely reported as about five miles -> "reportedly") and caught him. At Tonbridge/Tunbridge Wells petty sessions he faced four counts:
using a locomotive without a horse, having fewer than three persons in charge, driving over 2 mph, and not showing his name and address;
the speeding count was fined one shilling plus costs (sources give the total of all counts differently, so only "one shilling, plus costs"
is used). The Locomotives on Highways Act 1896 raised the limit to 14 mph (local authorities could set 12) and motorists marked it with the
14 November 1896 "Emancipation Run" from London to Brighton, commemorated every November by the London to Brighton Veteran Car Run.
Called "the first speeding ticket" (Guinness: first person charged with speeding); strictly it was a summons, so the hook keeps the popular phrasing.
